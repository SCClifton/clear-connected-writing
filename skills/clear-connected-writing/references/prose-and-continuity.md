# Prose and continuity

Use this guide when the draft's difficulty is its line of thought, paragraph flow or sentence rhythm. It is a set of diagnostic choices, not a required outline.

## Find the relationship before adding a transition

When two passages feel disconnected, state their relationship privately in ordinary language. Is the second explaining the first, testing its boundary, comparing an alternative, or showing a consequence? If that cannot be answered from the evidence, a transition word will only conceal the gap.

Carry forward a relevant idea the reader has just encountered, then introduce what changes. This can be a repeated technical term, a concise reference to a result, or a shared question. Do not reach for synonyms when consistency is needed to identify the same concept. Avoid vague references such as "this" or "these issues" when more than one antecedent is possible.

Use "therefore" only when the stated reasoning supports a consequence, and "however" only when there is a meaningful contrast. Sometimes an explicit comparison does the work better than a connector: "The second estimate includes installation labour, which the first excludes." Do not manufacture a causal link merely to produce smoother prose.

## Review the sequence with a reverse outline

For each paragraph, note its main contribution in a short phrase. Read those notes in order and look for an intelligible progression. Common problems include a definition arriving after its first use, a qualification separated from the claim it limits, several paragraphs repeating the same conclusion, or a new subject arriving without a reason.

Choose the progression that fits the question. An explanation may move from mechanism to consequence. A decision paper may begin with the decision and then explain the trade-off. A literature review may organise around competing explanations or different model boundaries. These are options, not universal structures.

At a major section boundary, orient the reader when the purpose changes. Briefly connect the preceding discussion to the new question and explain why it is now necessary. Do not add a miniature contents list or repeatedly tell the reader that the document will discuss something.

## Choose a useful paragraph opening

An opening can state a substantive claim, continue a comparison, introduce a definition, give an example or make a qualification. It should help the reader understand what follows and how it belongs. It does not need to be short, emphatic or evaluative every time.

Inspect a cluster of openings rather than applying a rule to each sentence in isolation. Repeated constructions such as "Cost matters", "Lifetime matters" and "Risk matters" can turn connected reasoning into a sequence of slogans. A useful revision often incorporates the specific reason: "Lifetime affects the estimate because the initial cost is spread over the output delivered before replacement."

Keep a direct short sentence when it carries real information. "The trial stopped after six hours" may be exactly the emphasis needed. Do not lengthen it just because neighbouring prose uses longer sentences.

## Develop enough explanation

A reader should be able to understand the claim, the relevant support and the interpretation that connects them. These functions may occupy one paragraph or several. Expand an explanation when it relies on unstated assumptions or specialist knowledge the intended reader is unlikely to have. Compress it when the conclusion has already been established or the detail does not affect understanding.

When a paragraph lists several results, explain their relationship rather than adding another result. When it contains only a conclusion, check whether the necessary support appears nearby. When it ends with a limitation, explain the consequence if that is needed to prevent the reader drawing too broad a conclusion.

Avoid ending every paragraph with "This is important because..." or "This supports the argument...". Establish the larger purpose where it belongs, then use specific explanations to carry that purpose through the section. Do not force every paragraph to close with a summary.

## Use sentence variation to express thought

Let length and syntax reflect the relationship between ideas. A longer sentence can hold a condition and its consequence together; a short one can mark a result or turning point. Combining two sentences is helpful when one merely announces the content of the other. Splitting is helpful when a sentence contains several relationships the reader must disentangle.

After combining, check subject and verb clarity, pronoun references and the number of qualifications. If the reader must retain several subordinate clauses before finding the main point, split or reorder the sentence. There is no target average sentence length or prescribed ratio of short to long sentences.

Read a representative passage aloud or silently at speaking pace. Listen for unnecessary stops, repeated openings, misplaced emphasis and sentences that cannot be understood on one reading. Keep intentional repetition when it supports definitions, instructions or comparison.

## Remove mechanical language without creating a new template

Replace vague evaluation with the actual reason. Instead of calling an approach "robust", state the condition it handled or the test it passed, if the evidence provides that information. Remove a claim if there is no supported substance behind the adjective.

Avoid canned contrasts, repeated three-item lists, decorative rhetorical questions and generic concluding summaries when they substitute for explanation. These forms are not inherently wrong; judge their contribution in context. Do not work from a universal blacklist or replace every familiar phrase with a more elaborate synonym.

Natural prose comes from a clear purpose, specific content and meaningful connections. It does not require randomness, deliberate mistakes, conversational filler or artificial irregularity.
