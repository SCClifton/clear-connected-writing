# Generic examples and calibration

All situations, values and labels below are invented for instruction. They are not evidence for a real project. The revised passages illustrate choices, not wording to reuse mechanically.

## Join an announcement to its explanation

Before:

> Lifetime matters. It affects the amount of output produced before replacement. This changes the cost per unit of output.

After:

> Lifetime affects the cost per unit of output because it determines how much output the system delivers before replacement.

The revision removes two unnecessary stops while retaining the relationship. If the surrounding argument already establishes this mechanism, further compression or deletion may be better than rewriting it.

## Keep a short sentence that does useful work

Before:

> The trial stopped after six hours. A seal had failed, so the remaining operating period could not be assessed.

After:

> The trial stopped after six hours. A seal had failed, so the remaining operating period could not be assessed.

No change is needed. The opening gives a concrete result and the next sentence explains its consequence. A rule requiring a longer opening would make the edit worse.

## Carry a comparison across paragraphs

Before:

> The model includes equipment and labour costs. It excludes replacement parts.
>
> Service life is important. Replacement costs can affect the total estimate.

After:

> The model includes equipment and labour costs but excludes replacement parts.
>
> That exclusion matters when the analysis period extends beyond the service life of components that need replacement, because their costs would fall within the period being assessed.

The second paragraph takes up the boundary introduced in the first. It states a condition instead of claiming that replacement always changes the result. If this were a source-based passage, that condition and interpretation would need appropriate support.

## Explain a comparison without inventing a ranking

Given invented source notes:

- Study A reports 80 currency units per item, excluding installation.
- Study B reports 110 currency units per item, including installation.
- Neither provides enough detail to put both estimates on the same basis.

Weak:

> Study A found a lower cost than Study B. This shows that its approach is more economical.

Better:

> Study A reports 80 currency units per item, compared with 110 in Study B, but only the second estimate includes installation. The reported difference therefore does not establish which approach has the lower installed cost. A common cost boundary is needed before these values can inform that comparison.

The revision explains what the figures do and do not establish. It does not attribute the entire difference to installation, because the notes do not isolate that effect. In a real review, retain the actual references next to their respective findings.

## Define a term at the point of use

Before:

> Utilisation is important. High utilisation reduces the fixed cost per operating hour.

After:

> For a fixed annual cost, spreading that cost over more operating hours reduces the cost per operating hour. In this example, utilisation means the share of available hours during which the equipment operates.

The definition is scoped to the example because conventions may differ. A technical audience familiar with the chosen definition might need a shorter explanation. Do not add an acronym unless it is useful and will recur.

## Make a professional request concrete

Before:

> It is important that we have strategic alignment on the proposed date. There are a number of dependencies that need to be considered. Please provide your feedback.

Given additional source information: the sender needs confirmation of a delivery date before booking an inspection.

After:

> Please confirm the delivery date so we can book the inspection. If the date is still uncertain, let us know what remains unresolved.

The improved request uses supplied context. Without that context, the editor should ask what decision or dependency matters, rather than inventing one.

## Preserve a personal voice

Before:

> Sorry I've been slow to reply. It's been a busy week. I'd still love to catch up if you're free on Sunday.

After:

> Sorry I've been slow to reply; it's been a busy week. I'd still love to catch up if you're free on Sunday.

This is an optional small rhythm change, not a necessary correction. The original may better match the writer's conversational voice. Do not replace it with a formal expression of regret, add an explanation for the busy week or turn the invitation into a scheduling checklist.

## Reject a smoother but stronger claim

Original:

> In the simulation, the revised schedule reduced average waiting time under the assumed demand profile.

Unacceptable revision:

> The revised schedule reduces waiting time and improves operational performance.

Acceptable revision:

> Under the assumed demand profile, the simulation produced a lower average waiting time with the revised schedule.

The unacceptable version removes the simulation boundary, generalises beyond the assumed conditions and adds an unsupported performance claim. Fluency cannot compensate for that loss of meaning.

## Forward-check the skill

When changing this skill, try realistic tasks using only invented or approved source material. Include a critique-only request, a short personal message, a technical comparison with unlike units or boundaries, and a passage that already reads well. Check whether the result follows the requested scope, preserves uncertainty and leaves effective prose alone. Evaluate the choices, not whether the output matches the examples word for word.
