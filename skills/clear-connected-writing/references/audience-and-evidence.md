# Audience, evidence and explanation

Read the part relevant to the task. Keep the method portable while adapting the voice and evidence standard to the actual reader.

## Academic and research writing

Organise a review around questions, mechanisms, methods, disagreements or boundaries. Use individual studies to develop those comparisons. A sequence of author-by-author summaries can be useful during extraction, but it rarely explains what the combined evidence establishes.

When comparing results, examine the basis of comparison before ranking them. Relevant differences may include the population or system studied, definition of the outcome, units and denominator, timescale, model boundary, price basis, experimental conditions, uncertainty and method. Select only the dimensions that affect interpretation in the passage.

Explain whether a difference reflects the underlying phenomenon, a modelling assumption, a measurement choice or an unresolved distinction. If the evidence does not isolate the cause, say so. Do not infer a historical trend from publication year alone or present values on different bases as directly comparable. Any conversion needs an explicit method and retained original values.

Prefer an original study for its specific findings, an authoritative review for a defensible field-level synthesis, and an appropriate textbook, standard or official method for an established definition. Source suitability depends on the claim. Peer review does not make a mismatched source appropriate, and a primary paper does not automatically support a broader generalisation.

Preserve the existing citation system. Attach citations to the claims they support, especially after merging or splitting sentences. A citation at the end of a paragraph must not appear to support new interpretation that the source does not establish. Check the original material before changing a substantive claim or reference when source checking is in scope. If it is unavailable, distinguish a retained citation from a verified one.

Keep the writer's synthesis visible as synthesis. Describe the specific unresolved question instead of using "more research is needed". Do not claim novelty, lack of evidence or a field-wide consensus from a limited search. Explain how a finding informs the present question without turning every paragraph into a repeated justification of the project.

## Technical explanation

Introduce a concept when the reader needs it, with enough mechanism to understand its use. Define an acronym at first substantive use when it will recur, then use it consistently. Avoid creating an acronym for a term used only once. Preserve discipline conventions where the intended audience already knows the notation.

For a metric, explain the question it answers, what enters the calculation, its units and what a higher or lower value means. State important exclusions. Related metrics may describe different aspects of the same system; do not imply that one determines another without the assumptions needed for that relationship.

Distinguish measured, calculated, modelled and assumed quantities. Preserve the difference between proposed, designed, ordered, built, tested and accepted states. A successful component test does not establish performance of an entire system unless the evidence supports that scope.

## Decisions and professional updates

Make the existing decision, request or result apparent early. Connect the evidence to its practical consequence and the action already proposed. Retain material alternatives, costs, conditions and uncertainty. Do not add deadlines, commitments, approval gates or promises that the source material does not contain.

Use technical detail where it changes the decision or helps the reader assess the explanation. Put background later if the reader can understand the request without it. A concise update may need only what changed, what it means and what happens next, but do not force that sequence onto every message.

Keep claims proportionate. Replace an unsupported assurance such as "delivery is on track" with the actual supported status. Do not convert an expected benefit into an achieved result for a stronger opening.

## Personal and interpersonal writing

Match the relationship, occasion and emotional purpose. Warmth may come from acknowledging a specific concern or expressing a clear intention, rather than from additional adjectives. Preserve natural contractions and informality when they fit the writer's voice.

Do not turn an apology, thank-you or family message into a business brief. A short personal note rarely needs citations, headings, a summary or an action list. Retain uncertainty or vulnerability when it is part of the writer's meaning. Do not invent feelings, motives, biographical details or shared memories.

## Figures, tables and worked examples

Suggest a visual when the reader must compare values, follow a mechanism, understand timing or see a trade-off that prose makes difficult. Identify the question the visual should answer before choosing a chart. Use a table for precise multidimensional comparison and a graph when the pattern or relationship is the point.

Separate reported data, derived quantities and illustrative assumptions. A worked example needs a stated purpose, inputs, calculation and interpretation. Label invented values as illustrative and avoid implying that they came from a publication. Explain the assumptions before making a general claim from the example.

Preserve units, denominators, baselines and uncertainty in visual comparisons. Do not compress unlike scenarios into one ranking. A caption should let the reader identify what is shown, its source and relevant conditions. The surrounding text should explain the takeaway, not recite every plotted value.

Creating or editing a visual requires suitable data, calculation and document tools beyond this language skill. Use them when that work is authorised; otherwise make a concrete suggestion without claiming to have created or verified the figure.
